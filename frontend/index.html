What is a moving average?

A moving average is the average closing price over the last 50 or 200 trading days. It smooths out daily noise so the trend is easier to see. When the price sits above its 200-day average, the market is usually described as being in an uptrend. Traders also watch for the 50-day line crossing the 200-day line. Treat these as descriptions of what has happened, not predictions.

Falls from the peak

How far below its previous high the price was on each day. Zero means a new high.

Momentum (RSI)

Compares the size of recent gains with recent losses on a 0–100 scale.

How is RSI used?

The Relative Strength Index looks at the last 14 trading days. Above 70 is often called "overbought" because prices rose fast, and below 30 "oversold". These are rules of thumb. An overbought market can keep rising for months, so RSI alone is not a reliable signal to buy or sell. DataVest will test how well it actually works in a later phase.

What is volatility?

Volatility measures how much the price jumps around from day to day, scaled to a year. Around 15–20% is normal for a broad stock index. Single companies are often 30% or more. Higher volatility means bigger swings both up and down, so you need to be able to sit through drops without panicking.

What is a drawdown?

A drawdown is how far the price fell from its highest point before recovering. The maximum drawdown is the worst of these. It answers a practical question: if you had bought at the worst possible moment, how much would you have been down on paper?

Why "per year"?

The compound annual growth rate (CAGR) is the steady yearly rate that would turn the starting value into the ending one. It makes periods of different lengths comparable. It hides the bumps, which is why you should read it next to volatility and drawdown.

What is Value at Risk?

Value at Risk (VaR) answers "how bad is a normal bad day?". The 95% one-day VaR is the daily fall that was only beaten on 1 day in 20. It says nothing about how bad the other day can be, which is why the average fall on those worst days (called expected shortfall) is shown too. Banks and funds use the same idea to set risk limits.
